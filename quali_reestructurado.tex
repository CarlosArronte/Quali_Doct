To illustrate the problem, consider a driver traveling along a highway at 100 km/h when it starts to rain. As visibility decreases, the driver decides to pull over onto the shoulder. During the maneuver, however, water on the road may reduce tire--road grip, making it harder to change course. What began as an attempt to stop the journey can, under these conditions, lead to a loss of control and a risk of collision with other vehicles or roadside barriers.

This example highlights a difficulty that extends beyond the driver's decision: the ability to execute a maneuver depends on the contact conditions between the tires and the road. When these conditions change, the forces available for braking or changing direction may also change. Understanding how grip varies along the route is therefore part of the problem of keeping the vehicle on its intended path.

Pavement wear and water on the road are among the factors that affect grip. Over time, traffic polishes the aggregates and changes the surface texture, reducing skid resistance \cite{Weng2026Leveraging,Isasa2025ASkid,wilson2006skidAnAnalysis}. During rainfall, water can impair tire--road contact and, under hydroplaning conditions, cause that contact to be lost \cite{Salleh2019Performance}. Ruts in the wheel paths allow water to accumulate, contributing to the problem \cite{Rajendra2013Development}. Both pavement wear and the conditions encountered during a journey can therefore alter the grip available for a maneuver.

This limitation becomes relevant when a driver needs to change lanes at short notice or swerve to avoid an obstacle. In these maneuvers, the lateral forces required to follow the intended path may exceed those that the tires can transmit to the road \cite{Bertipaglia2024Model}. The combination of high speed and low grip can then cause lateral sliding and yaw instability, leading to a loss of vehicle control \cite{Hu2025Lanechanging}. The challenge is therefore to execute the maneuver within the limits imposed by tire--road interaction.

Vehicle control systems address this challenge by assisting the driver with braking and maintaining vehicle stability. The anti-lock braking system (ABS) and electronic stability control (ESC) illustrate this development: the former aims to prevent wheel lock during braking, while the latter helps correct deviations between vehicle motion and the driver's intended direction. The introduction of ESP, an implementation of electronic stability control launched by Bosch in 1995, extended this assistance to situations involving a loss of stability \cite{Bosch2025ESP}. Development continues through the integration of braking, steering, and propulsion, whose coordinated operation draws on the capabilities of each system to regulate vehicle motion \cite{Skrickij2024Review}. For this assistance to respond to the situations encountered while driving, it remains necessary to account for how changes in grip affect the vehicle's ability to follow a path \cite{Hu2025Lanechanging}.

\chapter{Objectives}

\section{Overall objective}

Develop and evaluate a methodology for real-time validity estimation
for a hybrid vehicle dynamics model comprising a physical model and a
learned residual, and integrate this estimate into an MPC/NMPC controller
to reduce closed-loop performance degradation under operating conditions
not represented during training.

\section{Specific objectives}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{SO\arabic*:}]
    \item Formulate a hybrid vehicle dynamics architecture comprising a
    control-oriented physical model and a learned residual.

    \item Provide operational definitions of the types of shift/OOD
    relevant to the study, distinguishing at least unrepresented physical
    regimes and dynamics OOD, and including input OOD if time permits.

    \item Estimate and calibrate the aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty
    of the learned model using a heteroscedastic model and a small
    ensemble, complemented by a computationally inexpensive OOD indicator.

    \item Experimentally characterize the relationships among distribution
    shift, uncertainty, calibration, OOD, prediction error, and
    control-relevant error.

    \item Define a representation of runtime confidence/validity
    \[C_k=g(U_{\mathrm{epi}},U_{\mathrm{ale}},\mathrm{Calibration},
    \mathrm{OOD})\]
    and evaluate its ability to anticipate or
    represent model degradation in a way that is useful to the controller.

    \item Integrate the validity signal into an MPC/NMPC controller through
    horizon adaptation and evaluate its effect on closed-loop performance
    and empirical robustness.

    \item Compare the proposed approach with baseline architectures that
    isolate the effects of the learned model, validity estimation, and
    OOD detection.

    \item As an extension, if the schedule permits, evaluate the use of
    the validity signal in a CBF and/or a DAgger-based data acquisition
    and retraining strategy for conditions associated with epistemic
    uncertainty.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Research and validation methodology (Priority 1)}

\section{Overall strategy}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{S\arabic*:}]
    \item Establish the control-oriented physical model and the baseline
    MPC/NMPC controller.

    \item Train the learned residual and quantify its improvement and
    limitations relative to the physical model within the nominal domain.

    \item Implement aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty estimation,
    calibration, and a computationally inexpensive OOD score.

    \item Design physically interpretable shifts in vehicle dynamics and
    characterize their effects on prediction, uncertainty, OOD, and validity.

    \item Construct and validate the signal \(C_k\) against
    control-relevant error.

    \item Integrate \(C_k\) into MPC/NMPC adaptation.

    \item Compare the baselines and the proposed approach under nominal
    and OOD conditions.

    \item Evaluate empirical robustness and, if progress permits,
    incorporate CBF and/or DAgger extensions.
\end{enumerate}
