Consider a driver traveling along a highway at 100 km/h when it starts to rain. As visibility decreases, the driver decides to pull over onto the shoulder. During the maneuver, water on the road surface may reduce tire--road grip, making it more difficult to change direction. Under these conditions, what began as an attempt to stop may lead to a loss of control and a risk of collision with other vehicles or roadside barriers.

The difficulty in this example lies in executing a maneuver when the available grip changes. Steering and braking depend on forces transmitted through tire--road contact, so a change in these conditions may alter the vehicle's ability to follow the intended path \cite{Bertipaglia2024Model}.

Available grip varies with the pavement surface and the conditions encountered along the route. Pavement friction studies relate skid resistance to surface texture and road characteristics \cite{Weng2026Leveraging,Isasa2025ASkid}. Water on the road surface may impair tire--road contact, while ruts in the wheel paths may retain water and increase the risk of hydroplaning \cite{Salleh2019Performance}.

These limits become relevant when the driver needs to change lanes with little time to react or swerve to avoid an obstacle. The lateral forces required to follow the intended path may exceed those that tire--road contact can transmit \cite{Bertipaglia2024Model}. At high speeds and with low grip, this limitation may cause lateral sliding and yaw instability, compromising the maneuver \cite{Hu2025Lanechanging}.

Vehicle control systems assist the driver with braking and maintaining stability. The anti-lock braking system (ABS) aims to prevent wheel lock during braking \cite{BoschNDSafe}. Electronic stability control (ESC) helps correct deviations between vehicle motion and the direction intended by the driver; Bosch's electronic stability program (ESP), introduced in 1995, is an implementation of this function \cite{Bosch2025ESP}. Since then, development has extended to coordinating braking, steering, and propulsion. This coordination provides more ways to influence vehicle motion and requires information about its response, including states and parameters that may need to be estimated during operation \cite{Skrickij2024Review}.

The vehicle's response also depends on its mass, load, and tire properties. Friction and axle loads affect braking distance prediction \cite{Yun2026RealTime}, while mass and tire cornering stiffness enter the description of lateral and yaw motion \cite{Yeneneh2026Adaptive}. In the linear range, cornering stiffness is the slope of the relationship between tire lateral force and slip angle \cite{Skrickij2024Review}. It therefore describes an aspect of tire behavior distinct from the friction limit: the latter bounds the available forces, whereas stiffness influences how lateral force develops with slip angle. Variations in these quantities may affect both the forces available for a maneuver and the resulting motion.

Studies on adaptive vehicle control illustrate the consequences of these variations. Xu et al. evaluate path tracking through simulations on roads with low and variable friction, obtaining smaller lateral and heading errors with their adaptive strategy than with the comparison controllers \cite{Xu2026Parallel}. Yeneneh et al. use runtime parameter estimation to update a lateral tracking model that includes mass and cornering stiffness. In their simulations, the adaptive controller reduces tracking errors relative to conventional predictive control and a linear quadratic regulator \cite{Yeneneh2026Adaptive}. These results motivate considering changes in both the road environment and the vehicle itself, and examining whether the dynamics description used for control remains representative.

Predictive controllers use a model to relate control inputs to the expected vehicle response. Model predictive control (MPC), including its nonlinear formulation (NMPC), uses these predictions to select inputs over a finite horizon while accounting for constraints \cite{Su2026ReinforcementLearningBased}. The model must provide sufficient detail to support these decisions while keeping the control problem solvable with the available computational resources. Chen et al., for example, use a simplified bicycle model to balance prediction accuracy and computational cost \cite{Chen2026Hybrid}. These simplifications may introduce discrepancies between predicted and actual motion. Pan et al. identify model structure and parametric errors as sources of these discrepancies and account for them in a robust predictive controller \cite{Pan2026A}.

Uncertainty about a parameter's value does not imply that it is changing. A difference between its actual and assumed values may affect predictions even when its physical value remains constant. Yeneneh and Sufe consider uncertainty in mass, front and rear tire cornering stiffnesses, and road friction within a robust predictive control formulation \cite{Yeneneh2026AutoDyn}. Li et al. address uncertainty in cornering stiffness and yaw moment of inertia through a controller with longitudinal and lateral coupling, evaluated in hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) tests \cite{Li2026TrajectoryTracking}. These studies motivate accounting for parametric uncertainty when using a simplified model for control.

It is therefore useful to distinguish the vehicle's physical parameters, the nominal values used by the model, and estimates obtained from measurements during operation. Estimation may reduce parametric error, but it does not eliminate structural model approximations. Chen et al. reflect this distinction by combining calibration of a nominal physical model with compensation for the remaining unmodeled dynamics \cite{Chen2026DataMechanism}. Their formulation illustrates why improving prediction may require addressing discrepancies that parameter calibration alone does not capture.

Hybrid modeling addresses some of these discrepancies by combining physical equations with information learned from data \cite{Cheng2025Deep,Zhou2025Hybrid}. In a model with an additive learned residual, the physical component provides a nominal prediction and the learned component estimates a correction from the difference between that prediction and the observed response \cite{Kabzan2019LearningBased,Chen2026Hybrid}. The correction can represent effects that the simplified model does not describe adequately. In the formulation of Kabzan et al., training targets are obtained from measured state transitions and nominal predictions, allowing model error to be learned without separately identifying its physical causes \cite{Kabzan2019LearningBased}.

Kabzan et al. use Gaussian process regression for this correction in autonomous racing. During tests with a full-scale race car, the corrected model maintained an approximately constant mean prediction error as driving became more demanding over successive laps, whereas the nominal model's error increased \cite{Kabzan2019LearningBased}. Learning can also be assigned to individual force components. Zhou et al. combine a neural-network-based traction model with physical longitudinal dynamics and an estimator of vehicle mass and a modified rolling friction coefficient. Their evaluation includes loads and road conditions different from those used for training \cite{Zhou2025Hybrid}. This is a hybrid force-modeling and parameter-estimation architecture, in which learning plays a different role from that of an additive residual.

Other hybrid approaches fuse physical and neural models to predict dynamics or couple a neural estimator with a physical observer to estimate vehicle sideslip angle \cite{Cheng2025Deep,Bertipaglia2024An}. Physical knowledge can also guide training: Wang et al. include force-balance equation residuals in the learning objective of a vehicle dynamics model \cite{Wang2027Adaptive}. These uses of learning serve different purposes. This thesis focuses on an additive residual within the controller's prediction model, evaluated both for prediction accuracy and for its effect on control.

Consider, as a conceptual example, a residual trained with a particular vehicle load and subsequently used on a trip with additional passengers or cargo. If the nominal model and residual remain unchanged, the learned correction may cease to describe the discrepancy under the new load. Changes in tire properties may pose a similar difficulty: familiar speeds and steering inputs need not produce the same response when the underlying parameters differ \cite{Yeneneh2026AutoDyn}.

Load may vary between trips, and tire characteristics may evolve during driving. Kalaria et al. learn and update a tire model during operation to account for changes in lateral slip behavior; their evaluation uses numerical and high-fidelity simulators and includes simulated changes intended to represent wear \cite{Kalaria2024Adaptive}. More generally, Brunke et al. identify data scarcity and distribution shifts as practical challenges in learning-based control \cite{Brunke2022Safe}. A change does not necessarily invalidate a learned residual, but performance under some conditions does not by itself establish its usefulness under others. Chen et al. identify the limited ability of a correction trained before operation to adapt to new conditions as a motivation for updating it during operation \cite{Chen2026Hybrid}.

The research challenge is to assess during operation whether the residual continues to provide useful information for control when the vehicle response changes, and to determine how this assessment should influence the controller. This requires relating model error to the decisions it affects, without necessarily identifying the physical cause of the discrepancy.


\section{Research Problem}

The problem is studied using a control architecture that distinguishes the reference plant from the internal models considered by the controller. CarSim represents the vehicle's high-fidelity response, whereas the MPC must operate with descriptions sufficiently compact to make predictions and optimize steering actions at runtime. The starting point is a nominal linear bicycle model, denoted B0.

The research is not limited to training a single residual model. Several hybrid and supervisory configurations, denoted B1, B2, B3, and B4, are built from B0 and compared to analyze different ways of learning, evaluating, and using the discrepancy between the physical model and the plant. As a basic example, B1 retains the physical structure of B0 and incorporates a neural residual correction learned from CarSim data. Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_mpc_system} illustrates this configuration and summarizes the relationship between the reference plant, the hybrid model, and the controller. The description and formulation of B0--B4 will be discussed in Chapter 3, while their parameters, data, and training procedures will be specified in Chapter 7.

Within the domain represented by the training data, residual models may improve the accuracy of B0. However, this improvement does not guarantee that each correction remains useful when the operating distribution changes. Variations in grip, mass or load, and tire cornering stiffness, as well as speeds not represented during training or sensor degradation, may modify the learned relationships between the state, the steering input, and the model--plant discrepancy.

The central problem arises because the MPC may continue using learned information even after its predictive validity has decreased. B1 illustrates the basic case: a residual correction alone does not indicate whether it remains reliable for control decisions. The same problem must be examined in the other configurations, B2--B4. A degraded prediction may alter the optimized action sequence and impair closed-loop performance. Accordingly, the research addresses how to characterize the validity of learned models during operation, how to relate it to control-relevant error, and how to make the MPC respond when this validity decreases.

This thesis focuses on vehicle lateral dynamics and path tracking using an MPC/NMPC controller. The prediction model combines a control-oriented physical model with a residual learned from data. Within this architecture, the study will investigate runtime estimation of the validity of the learned correction and its use to adapt the controller when conditions differ from those represented during training. The families of change considered comprise variations in grip, mass or load, and tire cornering stiffness, as well as operation outside the range of states, speeds, or inputs used to train the residual. The methodology will select representative scenarios from these families, without attempting to cover all combinations or necessarily identify the physical parameter responsible for degradation.

The experimental protocol will distinguish measured variables, estimated states or parameters, and quantities unavailable to the controller. The kinematic signals and steering input required by the model will be considered available through measurement or estimation, as appropriate. In contrast, the true values of parameters such as grip, mass, inertias, or cornering stiffness may be used to configure and analyze scenarios without being supplied to the validity estimator or controller when the experiment defines them as unknown. The scope does not include perception, object detection, global planning, prediction of other agents, or guarantees for the complete autonomous system; in particular, the loss of visibility in the opening example is not a perception problem addressed by the thesis. The main contribution will be evaluated through predictive and closed-loop performance and empirical robustness criteria, without promising formal safety or stability guarantees.

\section{Vehicle Dynamics Relevant to Control}

The lateral motion considered in this thesis is described by a linear dynamic bicycle model. This representation combines the wheels on each axle into a single equivalent wheel and assumes small slip angles, linear cornering stiffnesses, and constant longitudinal speed around an operating point. For path tracking, the model is augmented with lateral and heading errors, allowing the relevant states to be represented without reproducing the full multibody dynamics of the high-fidelity plant. The resulting state vector is
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        e_y & e_\psi & v_y & r
    \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}},
    \label{eq:bicycle_states}
\end{equation}
%
where $e_\psi = \psi-\psi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.
The control input is the effective front-wheel steering angle, $\delta$, while the reference path curvature, $\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}$, is a known input.

Figure~\ref{fig:bicycle_model} illustrates the vehicle representation and the variables associated with tracking the reference path.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{imgs/Gemini_bicicleta.png}
    \caption{Schematic of the bicycle model and the variables used to describe lateral motion and path tracking.}
    \label{fig:bicycle_model}
\end{figure}

The model equations are
%
\begin{align}
    \dot{e}_y
    &= V_{x,0}e_\psi + v_y,
    \label{eq:bicycle_ey}\\
    %
    \dot{e}_\psi
    &= r - V_{x,0}\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}},
    \label{eq:bicycle_epsi}\\
    %
    \dot{v}_y
    &= -\frac{C_f+C_r}{mV_{x,0}}v_y
    + \left(
        \frac{C_rl_r-C_fl_f}{mV_{x,0}}-V_{x,0}
      \right)r
    + \frac{C_f}{m}\delta,
    \label{eq:bicycle_vy}\\
    %
    \dot{r}
    &= \frac{C_rl_r-C_fl_f}{I_zV_{x,0}}v_y
    - \frac{C_fl_f^2+C_rl_r^2}{I_zV_{x,0}}r
    + \frac{C_fl_f}{I_z}\delta.
    \label{eq:bicycle_r}
\end{align}

The cornering stiffnesses $C_f$ and $C_r$ represent equivalent values per axle. Lateral forces are approximated linearly, without explicit grip saturation.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{States and inputs of the augmented bicycle model.}
    \label{tab:bicycle_variables}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
        \toprule
        Symbol & Description & Unit \\
        \midrule
        $e_y$ & Lateral path error & $\mathrm{m}$ \\
        $e_\psi$ & Heading error & $\mathrm{rad}$ \\
        $v_y$ & Lateral velocity at the center of gravity & $\mathrm{m/s}$ \\
        $r$ & Yaw rate & $\mathrm{rad/s}$ \\
        $\delta$ & Effective front-wheel steering angle & $\mathrm{rad}$ \\
        $\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}$ & Reference path curvature & $\mathrm{m}^{-1}$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Mass, yaw moment of inertia, center of gravity position, and cornering stiffnesses determine the lateral and yaw response. In particular, the stiffnesses $C_f$ and $C_r$ describe the local slope of the force--slip angle relationship and should not be confused with the force limit imposed by tire--road grip. When the vehicle operates near saturation, its load changes, or it moves away from the nominal speed operating point, the linear assumptions may no longer adequately represent the plant response.

\section{Control-Oriented Models and Model Discrepancies}
\label{sec:modelos_orientados_control}

For control purposes, the preceding equations are organized as a state-space model. This formulation preserves the dominant relationships between steering, lateral motion, and yaw, and yields a discrete model compatible with the controller's update rate.

From these equations, the continuous state-space representation is
%
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{x}}
    =
    \mathbf{A}_c\mathbf{x}
    + \mathbf{B}_c\delta
    + \mathbf{E}_c\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}},
    \label{eq:bicycle_continuous}
\end{equation}
%
with
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}_c =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 & V_{x,0} & 1 & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
        0 & 0
          & -\dfrac{C_f+C_r}{mV_{x,0}}
          & \dfrac{C_rl_r-C_fl_f}{mV_{x,0}}-V_{x,0} \\
        0 & 0
          & \dfrac{C_rl_r-C_fl_f}{I_zV_{x,0}}
          & -\dfrac{C_fl_f^2+C_rl_r^2}{I_zV_{x,0}}
    \end{bmatrix},
    \label{eq:bicycle_Ac}
\end{equation}
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{B}_c =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 \\
        0 \\
        C_f/m \\
        C_fl_f/I_z
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{E}_c =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 \\
        -V_{x,0} \\
        0 \\
        0
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:bicycle_Bc_Ec}
\end{equation}

For use in MPC prediction, the model is discretized using a \textit{zero-order hold} (ZOH), assuming constant inputs over each sampling interval:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{k+1}
    =
    \mathbf{A}_d\mathbf{x}_k
    + \mathbf{B}_d\delta_k
    + \mathbf{E}_d\kappa_{\mathrm{ref},k},
    \label{eq:bicycle_discrete}
\end{equation}
%
where
%
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{A}_d
    &= \exp\left(\mathbf{A}_cT_s\right),
    \label{eq:bicycle_Ad}\\
    %
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{B}_d & \mathbf{E}_d
    \end{bmatrix}
    &=
    \int_0^{T_s}
        \exp\left(\mathbf{A}_c\tau\right)
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mathbf{B}_c & \mathbf{E}_c
        \end{bmatrix}
    \,\mathrm{d}\tau.
    \label{eq:bicycle_Bd_Ed}
\end{align}

The matrices are calculated at the nominal speed $V_{x,0}$ and remain constant throughout the simulation. This linear internal model, consisting solely of the bicycle model, is denoted B0 and constitutes the physical basis into which the learned correction is incorporated.

The simplification that makes B0 computationally useful also causes discrepancies relative to CarSim. Sources include tire linearization, omitted saturation and coupling effects, errors in nominal parameters, and changes in operating regime. An unknown but constant actual parameter can produce model error without constituting a distribution shift; a distribution shift occurs when operation departs from the conditions represented by the data or the assumptions used to construct the model.

\section{Hybrid Models: Physics and Learned Residuals}
\label{sec:modelos_hibridos_residuales}

A hybrid model retains the physical prediction as its underlying structure and adds a learned function to approximate the discrepancy that this structure does not explain. In general form,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{k+1}
    =
    f_{\mathrm{phys}}(\mathbf{x}_k,\mathbf{u}_k)
    +
    \mathbf{r}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_k,\mathbf{u}_k)
    +
    \mathbf{w}_k,
    \label{eq:generic_hybrid_model}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\mathrm{phys}}$ is the nominal model, $\mathbf{r}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is the learned residual, and $\mathbf{w}_k$ groups disturbances and unrepresented discrepancies. In architecture B1, this idea is specialized to the lateral states of the bicycle model.

To complement the physical description of B0, a residual model is incorporated to estimate prediction discrepancies in lateral velocity and yaw rate. The combination of the bicycle model and this learned correction is denoted B1. Its purpose is to represent part of the dynamics not captured by the nominal model's approximations, while retaining the physical structure as the basis for prediction.

The lateral state vector is defined as
%
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\xi}_k =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        v_{y,k} \\
        r_k
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:b1_lateral_states}
\end{equation}
%
The physical prediction of these states is obtained through
%
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathrm{B0}}_{k+1|k}
    =
    \mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{lat}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_k
    + \mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{lat}}\delta_k,
    \label{eq:b1_physical_prediction}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{lat}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}$ and $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{lat}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times1}$ are the submatrices of $\mathbf{A}_d$ and $\mathbf{B}_d$ corresponding to states $v_y$ and $r$, respectively.

Once the physical prediction has been defined, the residual used as the training target is obtained as the difference between the CarSim response at the next instant and the one-step physical prediction:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{d}_k
    =
    \boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathrm{CarSim}}_{k+1}
    -
    \hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathrm{B0}}_{k+1|k}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        d_{v_y,k} \\
        d_{r,k}
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:b1_residual_target}
\end{equation}
%
Both responses are compared for the same transition, using the corresponding initial state and steering input. The prediction interval matches the controller's sampling period, $T_s$. Therefore, the residual components have units of $\mathrm{m/s}$ and $\mathrm{rad/s}$: they represent discrete corrections to the states, not to their derivatives.

This residual is approximated using a \textit{multilayer perceptron} (MLP) neural network, whose input vector is
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_k =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        V_{x,k} & v_{y,k} & r_k & \delta_k
    \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.
    \label{eq:b1_features}
\end{equation}
%
The network provides a deterministic estimate of the two residual components:
%
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{d}}_k
    =
    \mathbf{g}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_k),
    \label{eq:b1_residual_mapping}
\end{equation}
%
where $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ groups its weights and biases. Although measured longitudinal speed is included among the network inputs, the physical model matrices remain fixed at the nominal operating point.

Once the residual has been trained, the lateral prediction of the hybrid model is constructed by adding the learned correction to the B0 prediction:
%
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathrm{B1}}_{k+1|k}
    =
    \mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{lat}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_k
    +
    \mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{lat}}\delta_k
    +
    \mathbf{g}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_k).
    }
    \label{eq:b1_hybrid_prediction}
\end{equation}
%
The residual is added directly to the physical prediction, without multiplying it by $T_s$ again, because it already represents a discrepancy over one sampling interval.

The validity of this correction depends on the domain covered by the data. Outside that domain, a deterministic residual may produce a numerically plausible correction without indicating that its error has increased. This limitation motivates estimating uncertainty, calibration, and distance from the training domain during operation, and relating these signals to the effect of error on control.

\section{Model Predictive Control}

MPC computes an input sequence using a model to predict system evolution over a finite horizon and explicitly account for its constraints \cite{Su2026ReinforcementLearningBased}. For a prediction horizon $N$, a representative formulation is
\begin{align}
    \min_{\mathbf{u}_{0|k},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{N-1|k}}\quad
    & \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}
      \left(
        \lVert \mathbf{x}_{i|k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{ref}}_{i|k}\rVert_{\mathbf{Q}}^2
        +
        \lVert \mathbf{u}_{i|k}\rVert_{\mathbf{R}}^2
      \right)
      +
      \lVert \mathbf{x}_{N|k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{ref}}_{N|k}\rVert_{\mathbf{P}}^2,
    \label{eq:mpc_cost}\\
    \text{subject to}\quad
    & \mathbf{x}_{i+1|k}
      =
      f_{\mathrm{pred}}(\mathbf{x}_{i|k},\mathbf{u}_{i|k}), \nonumber\\
    & \mathbf{x}_{i|k}\in\mathcal{X},\qquad
      \mathbf{u}_{i|k}\in\mathcal{U}. \nonumber
\end{align}
The matrices $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{R}$, and $\mathbf{P}$ weight tracking, control effort, and the terminal state; $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{U}$ represent the constraints. If $f_{\mathrm{pred}}$ is linear, the result is a linear MPC formulation; if it retains relevant nonlinearities, the problem corresponds to NMPC.

Only the first input of the optimized sequence is applied to the plant. At the next instant, the state is updated, the horizon is shifted, and the problem is solved again. This receding-horizon principle allows new information to be incorporated, but does not eliminate dependence on model quality: a persistent error in $f_{\mathrm{pred}}$ propagates along the horizon and may modify both the predicted cost and constraint activation.

In this thesis, the horizon $N$ is the main candidate variable for adapting the controller based on the validity signal. The direction and magnitude of this adaptation are not assumed in advance: they must be determined by analyzing the trade-off between predictive reach, model error accumulation, closed-loop performance, and computational cost.

\section{Learned Information Relevant to the Controller}
\label{sec:informacion_aprendida_control}

% La información aprendida principal de la tesis NO es una taxonomía
% genérica de salidas de IA. Es el residual/model mismatch incorporado
% al modelo de predicción del controlador.
%
% Situar otros roles funcionales de modelos aprendidos solo como contexto
% bibliográfico: full learned dynamics, learned tire/force models,
% estimadores de parámetros, etc.

Information produced by a learned model is relevant to control when it enters the representation of dynamics, state estimation, or a controller decision. Learning can be used, for example, to represent forces or dynamics components, estimate states or parameters, and correct nominal model predictions. These functions are not equivalent: a learned force model introduces a specific description of a subsystem, an estimator provides a physical quantity or state, and a residual model directly approximates the prediction discrepancy without separating its causes \cite{Zhou2025Hybrid,Bertipaglia2024An,Kabzan2019LearningBased}.

In this thesis, the main learned information is the discrete residual defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:b1_residual_target}. From the variables available in $\mathbf{z}_k$, the learned model estimates the correction $\hat{\mathbf{d}}_k$ to lateral velocity and yaw rate. This correction is incorporated into the transition used by the MPC/NMPC, according to Equation~\eqref{eq:b1_hybrid_prediction}. Thus, the residual is neither a control action nor a direct estimate of grip, mass, or tire cornering stiffness: it represents the combined effect of structural simplifications, parametric errors, and other discrepancies on the one-step prediction. This formulation allows model error to be learned without separately identifying each physical cause, as in other residual learning approaches for predictive control \cite{Kabzan2019LearningBased,Chen2026Hybrid}.

The architecture thus distinguishes three levels of information. Actual parameters belong to the plant and may change between scenarios; nominal parameters determine physical model B0; and measured or estimated signals form the inputs available for computing predictions. In the proposed protocol, $V_x$, $v_y$, $r$, and $\delta$ are available to the residual model, whereas grip and the parameters characterizing mass, inertias, or tires are not supplied as inputs when the experiment defines them as unknown. Consequently, a latent physical change may modify the model--plant discrepancy even when the observed variables remain within familiar ranges.

The relevance of the residual is not determined solely by its instantaneous error. As part of the transition model, its corrections may propagate along the prediction horizon and modify the input sequence selected by the controller. In probabilistic configurations, the residual prediction is also accompanied by a characterization of its dispersion or model uncertainty. These additional signals do not replace the residual or constitute a validity measure on their own; their purpose is to provide evidence for assessing whether the learned correction remains adequate for control decisions. The following sections clarify the interpretation of uncertainty, distribution shifts, and runtime validity.

\section{Predictive Uncertainty}
\label{sec:incertidumbre_predictiva}

A point prediction of the residual indicates the correction that the model considers most likely, but does not express how much dispersion it assigns to that estimate. Probabilistic models represent each component $d_{j,k}$ through a predictive distribution conditioned on the input and the training data,
\begin{equation}
    p\!\left(d_{j,k}\mid\mathbf{z}_k,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}\right),
    \qquad j\in\{v_y,r\}.
    \label{eq:predictive_residual_distribution}
\end{equation}
In the configurations studied, this distribution is described by a mean $\mu_{j,k}$ and a predictive variance. To facilitate analysis, the total variance is operationally decomposed as
\begin{equation}
    U^2_{\mathrm{tot},j,k}
    =
    U^2_{\mathrm{ale},j,k}
    +
    U^2_{\mathrm{epi},j,k},
    \label{eq:predictive_uncertainty_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $U_{\mathrm{ale}}$ represents the conditional dispersion attributed to random variability and $U_{\mathrm{epi}}$ the uncertainty associated with model knowledge. This separation depends on the model and estimation procedure: its components are not directly observed physical quantities, nor are they uniquely identified from the data.

Predictive uncertainty is used here as supervisory information for the residual, rather than as a synonym for error. A high standard deviation does not demonstrate that the mean is incorrect, and a small deviation does not guarantee that the prediction is valid. Its usefulness must be established by comparing it with observed errors, distribution shifts, and, ultimately, the consequences of those errors for the controller.

\subsection{Aleatoric Uncertainty}

% Definición, interpretación y papel dentro del modelo heteroscedástico.
% Relacionar con ruido/variabilidad no reducible bajo los supuestos del
% modelo, evitando equipararla automáticamente con invalidity.

Aleatoric uncertainty describes the variability remaining in the output after conditioning the prediction on the variables supplied to the model. It may encompass measurement noise, unobserved disturbances, and plant variations that the selected inputs cannot explain. It is considered irreducible under the available representation and information, although it may change if new variables are incorporated, the model is modified, or the measurement system improves. For this reason, the separation between aleatoric uncertainty and modeling error must be interpreted relative to the adopted assumptions, rather than as an absolute classification \cite{Brunke2022Safe}.

When constant dispersion is assumed throughout the input space, the model is homoscedastic. This assumption is used in B2, where the term $\sigma_{n,j}^{2}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:b2_gp_observation_model} is a single value for each residual component. A heteroscedastic model instead allows dispersion to depend on $\mathbf{z}_k$:
\begin{equation}
    d_{j,k}
    =
    \mu_j(\mathbf{z}_k)
    +
    \varepsilon_{j,k},
    \qquad
    \varepsilon_{j,k}\sim
    \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,U^2_{\mathrm{ale},j}(\mathbf{z}_k)\right).
    \label{eq:heteroscedastic_residual_model}
\end{equation}
B3 learns this dispersion together with the mean using a heteroscedastic Gaussian loss function, whereas B4 estimates it in a second stage from out-of-fold residuals. In both cases, $U_{\mathrm{ale}}$ should be read as an operational estimate of residual dispersion. It may contain not only physical noise, but also mean-model discrepancies and unexplained variability between runs.

A region with high conditional dispersion may still belong to the training domain, and an unobserved physical change may produce systematic error without increasing this component. Consequently, aleatoric uncertainty provides information about the expected magnitude of errors, but does not by itself establish that the residual has lost validity.

\subsection{Epistemic Uncertainty}

% Definición e interpretación como incertidumbre asociada al conocimiento
% del modelo/datos. Introducir el small ensemble como mecanismo candidato.
% Relacionarla con regiones poco representadas, pero no afirmar que
% epistemic uncertainty sea equivalente a OOD.

Epistemic uncertainty represents the lack of knowledge associated with finite data, insufficient domain coverage, and ambiguity in model fitting. Unlike the aleatoric component, it can in principle be reduced through informative observations in the relevant regions, although the actual reduction depends on the ability of the model family to represent the dynamics \cite{Brunke2022Safe}.

This component is approximated in two ways in the probabilistic configurations. Gaussian processes B2 and B4 use the posterior standard deviation of the latent function, which depends on the location of the query relative to the training data and on the selected kernel. B3 uses disagreement among ensemble member means:
\begin{equation}
    U^2_{\mathrm{epi},j,k}
    =
    \frac{1}{M}
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \left(
        \mu^{(m)}_{j,k}-\bar{\mu}_{j,k}
    \right)^2,
    \qquad
    \bar{\mu}_{j,k}
    =
    \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mu^{(m)}_{j,k}.
    \label{eq:ensemble_epistemic_uncertainty}
\end{equation}
Disagreement reflects sensitivity to different solutions obtained during training, but does not cover every source of missing knowledge. Members may share data, architecture, and representation biases and therefore agree on an erroneous prediction.

$U_{\mathrm{epi}}$ is expected to increase in sparsely represented regions, but this response is not guaranteed for every change. In particular, a change in grip, mass, or tires not included in $\mathbf{z}_k$ may alter the input--output relationship without visibly shifting the inputs. Epistemic uncertainty and out-of-distribution detection are therefore related but distinct concepts: a distant input may not cause a relevant error, and a latent dynamic change may produce a confident failure.

\subsection{Calibration}

% Explicar por qué no basta con producir una varianza: la incertidumbre
% debe contrastarse con la frecuencia/magnitud real de los errores.
% Definir las métricas de calibration que finalmente se utilicen.

Calibration compares predictive distributions with subsequently observed errors. For a Gaussian approximation with standard deviation $U_{\mathrm{tot},j,k}$, a two-sided interval with nominal level $1-\alpha$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_{j,k}^{1-\alpha}
    =
    \left[
        \mu_{j,k}-q_{1-\alpha/2}U_{\mathrm{tot},j,k},
        \mu_{j,k}+q_{1-\alpha/2}U_{\mathrm{tot},j,k}
    \right],
    \label{eq:predictive_interval}
\end{equation}
where $q_{1-\alpha/2}$ is the corresponding standard normal quantile. Empirical coverage over $N$ observations is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}_{j}^{1-\alpha}
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{k=1}^{N}
    \mathbb{I}\!\left\{
        d_{j,k}\in\mathcal{I}_{j,k}^{1-\alpha}
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:empirical_predictive_coverage}
\end{equation}
Coverage below the nominal level indicates overconfidence, whereas higher coverage may indicate conservative intervals. However, coverage alone does not reward sharpness: unnecessarily wide intervals may cover many observations while providing little information.

For this reason, the Gaussian negative log-likelihood is also used, combining mean error with the declared dispersion:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NLL}_j
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{k=1}^{N}
    \left[
        \frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(2\pi U^2_{\mathrm{tot},j,k}\right)
        +
        \frac{\left(d_{j,k}-\mu_{j,k}\right)^2}
             {2U^2_{\mathrm{tot},j,k}}
    \right].
    \label{eq:gaussian_predictive_nll}
\end{equation}
Joint evaluation of coverage and NLL can reveal both overconfidence and excessive dispersion. When the prediction comes from a mixture, as in ensemble B3, it must be stated whether the NLL corresponds to the exact mixture or to a moment-matched Gaussian approximation.

Calibration must be checked on partitions not used to fit the model and broken down by region and family of change. Adequate average coverage within the nominal domain may hide local errors and does not guarantee that intervals remain calibrated when the dynamics change. Furthermore, calibrating one-step residual error does not demonstrate that the uncertainty represents accumulated error over the horizon or its closed-loop effect. Calibration therefore provides a quality condition for interpreting probabilistic signals, but is not a sufficient definition of validity for control.

\section{Distribution Shifts and OOD Detection in Vehicle Dynamics}

% Separar explícitamente las categorías operacionales elegidas.

The training domain is not defined solely by the ranges of individual input variables. It also encompasses combinations of states and inputs, the maneuvers generating those combinations, and the dynamic relationship between model inputs and the observed residual. To express this distinction, the joint distribution can be factorized as
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{d})
    =
    p(\mathbf{d}\mid\mathbf{z})\,p(\mathbf{z}),
    \label{eq:joint_shift_factorization}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}$ contains the variables available to the predictor and $\mathbf{d}$ is the model--plant discrepancy. During operation, the input distribution $p(\mathbf{z})$, the conditional relationship $p(\mathbf{d}\mid\mathbf{z})$, or both may change. This separation matters because a detector based exclusively on inputs can identify departure from the observed domain, but not necessarily a dynamics change conditioned on a parameter that the model does not receive.

In this work, the term out of distribution is always used relative to an explicit reference domain and a specific representation. It is not considered an absolute property of a sample. Furthermore, detecting a distribution shift does not demonstrate that the residual has lost usefulness, just as a large error does not require statistically atypical inputs. The OOD indicator is intended to provide a departure signal that must be compared with predictive error and its effect on control. This caution is especially relevant in learning-based control, where distribution shifts and data scarcity may affect generalization without independently determining the closed-loop consequence \cite{Brunke2022Safe}.

\subsection{Unrepresented Physical Regime}
\label{sec:regimen_fisico_no_representado}

% Caso principal: cambios de parámetros/condiciones físicas no presentes
% durante entrenamiento: variaciones de fricción, masa/carga o rigidez
% lateral. Definir para cada caso el dominio de entrenamiento y la
% información disponible; no equiparar parámetro desconocido con OOD.

A physical regime is characterized by tire--road contact conditions and the parameters determining the vehicle response. In compact form, these quantities can be grouped as
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\vartheta}_k
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mu_k & m_k & I_{z,k} & C_{f,k} & C_{r,k} & \cdots
    \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T},
    \label{eq:physical_regime_vector}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ represents grip, $m$ mass, $I_z$ yaw moment of inertia, and $C_f$ and $C_r$ equivalent cornering stiffnesses. An unrepresented physical regime occurs when the configuration used during operation does not belong to the set of configurations used to generate the training data. The definition therefore depends on which parameters were varied and which ranges were covered in that set.

An unknown parameter must be distinguished from an unrepresented parameter. If a physical value remains fixed during both training and operation, the residual may learn part of the discrepancy it produces even if the controller does not know that value. There is parametric uncertainty relative to the physical model, but not necessarily a distribution shift. Conversely, if grip, mass, load, or tire properties depart from the configurations used to train the residual, the relationship between $\mathbf{z}_k$ and $\mathbf{d}_k$ may change. Studies on adaptive control and modeling show that these quantities play different roles in vehicle response and that their variation may require adapting the model or controller \cite{Yeneneh2026AutoDyn,Kalaria2024Adaptive,Zhou2025Hybrid}.

In this thesis architecture, the actual values of $\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_k$ are used to configure the reference plant and describe experimental scenarios, but are not supplied to the residual when the protocol declares them unavailable. Thus, a reduction in grip or a change of vehicle may constitute a latent physical change: the predictor receives $V_x$, $v_y$, $r$, and $\delta$, but no label directly identifying the new configuration. The objective is not to recover each component of $\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_k$, but to determine whether the learned correction continues to describe the discrepancy usefully for the controller.

Not every physical change implies a loss of validity. A moderate variation may have a small effect on the corrected states or be partially accommodated by residual generalization. Scenarios must therefore be organized by family and severity and evaluated against a nominal reference. The unrepresented-regime label identifies the experimental cause of change; residual degradation and its control relevance must be measured separately.

\subsection{Out-of-Distribution Dynamics}

% Regiones de estado/dinámica nunca vistas o insuficientemente cubiertas
% durante entrenamiento. Ejemplo de escenario: V_x > V_{x,\max}^{train}
% o regiones dinámicas no representadas.
% Aclarar el posible solapamiento con cambios físicos: un cambio de masa
% o neumáticos puede alterar la dinámica aun con estados/entradas familiares.
% Definir categorías y etiquetas experimentales sin asumir que un detector
% basado solo en entradas identificará todos esos cambios.

Out-of-distribution dynamics refers to a transition or operating region whose relationship between state, input, and response is not sufficiently represented by the training data. In terms of Equation~\eqref{eq:joint_shift_factorization}, the most direct case occurs when
\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{d}\mid\mathbf{z})
    \neq
    p_{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{d}\mid\mathbf{z}),
    \label{eq:conditional_dynamics_shift}
\end{equation}
for a region relevant to the maneuver. The inequality represents an operational difference to be quantified through error and the selected indicators; it does not presume that the distributions are known analytically.

This situation can arise in two ways. In the first, the vehicle enters an observable region of the operating space not covered during training. For example, if the residual was fitted at a single longitudinal speed, operating below or above it requires extrapolation with respect to $V_x$, even if the physical configuration remains fixed. In the second, inputs may retain familiar values while a latent plant variable changes. A change in grip, mass, or tires may then produce a different residual for similar values of $\mathbf{z}_k$. The first case combines covariate shift with possible dynamic extrapolation; the second corresponds to a conditional shift that need not be visible in input space.

This distinction determines what each mechanism can detect. A distance or density indicator computed on $\mathbf{z}_k$ is suitable for flagging poorly covered state and input regions, but may remain low under a latent physical change. An indicator based on innovations or observed errors may respond to the latter, although only after the plant response becomes available, and may confuse dynamic change with noise or a sensor fault. Therefore, no single indicator is adopted as universal proof of OOD dynamics.

The methodology implements these categories through separate scenarios. Speed changes represent observable extrapolations of the operating point; variations in grip and vehicle configuration represent latent dynamic changes; and changes during a run allow the temporal response of the indicators to be studied. Tables~\ref{tab:experimental_campaign_configuration} and \ref{tab:experimental_campaign_purpose} document the nominal domain, available information, and planned comparison for each case. This classification allows whether a change was detected, whether it degraded prediction, and whether that degradation was relevant to control to be analyzed as related but distinct questions.

\section{Runtime Validity and Confidence}
\label{sec:validez_confianza_operacion}

% Sección central del marco conceptual.
%
% Establecer explícitamente:
%
% uncertainty != OOD != validity.
%
% Proponer una señal de trabajo:
%
% donde C_k no se define como ``probabilidad de estar correcto'' salvo
% que posteriormente exista una calibración que justifique esa lectura.
%
% La señal debe evaluarse por su relación con error relevante para
% control, no solo por AUROC de detección.
% Evaluar si detecta degradación sin conocer el parámetro que la causa;
% no interpretar C_k como una estimación de fricción, masa o rigidez lateral.

In this thesis, residual \emph{validity} is understood as its adequacy for the role it plays within the controller's prediction model under current conditions. A residual is operationally valid when its correction maintains sufficient quality to support MPC/NMPC decisions. This definition is relative to the task, horizon, and variables affecting control; it does not require the learned model to identify the physical cause of the discrepancy or reproduce every aspect of the plant with equal accuracy.

Four concepts should be distinguished. Predictive uncertainty expresses the dispersion the model assigns to its prediction; the OOD indicator characterizes departure from a reference domain; validity describes the residual's actual adequacy for its intended use; and confidence is an estimate of that validity available during operation, before the future plant response is known. In compact form,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{uncertainty}
    \neq
    \mathrm{OOD}
    \neq
    \mathrm{validity}
    \neq
    \mathrm{confidence}.
    \label{eq:uncertainty_ood_validity_confidence}
\end{equation}
The four notions may be related, but none automatically substitutes for the others. A point may lie far from the data and still admit a useful correction; a large, confident error may also occur under a latent dynamic change.

Runtime confidence is represented by a scalar signal $C_k$, normalized so that larger values indicate greater support for using the residual. The working formulation is
\begin{equation}
    C_k
    =
    g\!\left(
        U_{\mathrm{epi},k},
        U_{\mathrm{ale},k},
        \mathrm{Cal}_k,
        S_{\mathrm{OOD},k}
    \right),
    \qquad 0\leq C_k\leq 1,
    \label{eq:runtime_confidence_signal}
\end{equation}
where $S_{\mathrm{OOD},k}$ is the selected departure score and $\mathrm{Cal}_k$ represents the calibration information or transformation applicable to the probabilistic signals. The latter need not be a new instantaneous measurement: it may correspond to recalibration parameters obtained from a held-out set and, if the method allows, a local diagnostic. The form of $g$ is not fixed a priori. It must be determined after studying the sensitivity, redundancy, and complementarity of its components; if a signal adds no information, the final representation may omit it.

$C_k$ is not interpreted as the probability that the prediction is correct unless a validity event is defined and that probabilistic calibration is demonstrated empirically. In its initial formulation, it is an index of relative support. Nor does it estimate $\mu$, $m$, $C_f$, or $C_r$: it should respond to degradation without identifying which of those parameters changed. This separation allows the same mechanism to be evaluated across different families of change, although its thresholds and sensitivity may not be universal.

During operation, $U_{\mathrm{epi},k}$, $U_{\mathrm{ale},k}$, and $S_{\mathrm{OOD},k}$ can be computed from the current input before observing $\mathbf{d}_k$. Actual validity can only be assessed subsequently, when the plant transition or a closed-loop performance measure becomes available. The design of $C_k$ must therefore separate the data used to train the residual, calibrate the signals, select function $g$, and evaluate generalization. Without this separation, the index might describe the scenarios used to tune it without reliably estimating validity during new operation.

\section{Control-Relevant Validity}
\label{sec:validez_relevante_control}

% Diferenciar:
% - error estadístico del predictor;
% - error dinámico acumulado/multi-step;
% - error que altera la optimización del MPC/NMPC;
% - degradación closed-loop.
%
% Esta sección es una de las claves para demostrar profundidad doctoral.

One-step residual accuracy constitutes the first level of evaluation, but does not by itself determine control relevance. If $\mathbf{d}_k$ is the actual discrepancy relative to B0 and $\hat{\mathbf{d}}_k$ the learned correction, the physical and hybrid model errors can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e}^{\mathrm{B0}}_{d,k}=\mathbf{d}_k,
    \qquad
    \mathbf{e}^{\mathrm{hyb}}_{d,k}
    =
    \mathbf{d}_k-\hat{\mathbf{d}}_k.
    \label{eq:physics_hybrid_residual_errors}
\end{equation}
For a loss function $\ell_d$, the instantaneous predictive gain of the residual over the physical model is
\begin{equation}
    G^{(1)}_k
    =
    \ell_d\!\left(\mathbf{e}^{\mathrm{B0}}_{d,k}\right)
    -
    \ell_d\!\left(\mathbf{e}^{\mathrm{hyb}}_{d,k}\right).
    \label{eq:one_step_residual_gain}
\end{equation}
A positive value indicates that the correction improves one-step prediction; a negative value indicates that it is harmful for that transition. This measure allows checking whether learning adds value relative to B0, but does not yet capture error propagation or optimization sensitivity.

The second level is accumulated dynamic error. Applying the model recursively over a horizon $H$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e}_{x,i\mid k}
    =
    \mathbf{x}_{k+i}
    -
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+i\mid k},
    \qquad i=1,\ldots,H.
    \label{eq:multistep_state_prediction_error}
\end{equation}
Even a small one-step bias may accumulate, whereas errors of alternating sign may partially cancel. Furthermore, the controller itself modifies future inputs, so evaluating open-loop trajectories does not necessarily reproduce the state distribution visited in closed loop.

The third level is decision relevance. The MPC/NMPC weights states, inputs, and constraints through the cost function in Equation~\eqref{eq:mpc_cost}. Consequently, errors of equal statistical magnitude may have different effects: a discrepancy in a low-sensitivity direction may leave the first optimized action unchanged, while a smaller error near a constraint or during a demanding maneuver may alter the control sequence. A control-relevant error measure must therefore account for propagation over the horizon, problem weights, and proximity to constraints, rather than only the predictor's average RMSE.

The fourth level is observed closed-loop performance. Estimated validity is useful to this thesis only if using it reduces tracking degradation, constraint violations, or control cost under the changes studied while maintaining computational feasibility. The relationship to be evaluated is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \text{condition change}
        &\longrightarrow
        \text{residual error} \\
        &\longrightarrow
        \text{effect on optimization}
        \longrightarrow
        \text{closed-loop performance}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:validity_control_relevance_chain}
\end{equation}
This chain avoids defining validity solely through an OOD label or a probabilistic metric.

Evaluation of $C_k$ must check its association with all four levels: predictive gain over B0, multistep error, relevant changes in decisions, and closed-loop degradation. It must also include cases where a physical change does not harm the residual and cases of confident failure. This avoids rewarding a signal that responds to every change even when it does not matter to the controller. The specific MPC/NMPC adaptation is defined in the methodological chapters; conceptually, $C_k$ informs how much to trust the correction, but does not constitute a formal guarantee of stability, recursive feasibility, or safety.

\section{The Shift--Validity--Control Chain}
\label{sec:cadena_cambio_validez_control}

% Figura conceptual recomendada:
%
% operating-condition or vehicle-parameter shift
% -> learned residual
% -> {prediction, U_epi, U_ale, calibration, OOD}
% -> C_k
% -> MPC/NMPC adaptation
% -> u_k
% -> vehicle
% -> closed-loop metrics.

The proposed architecture is organized around a causal chain linking operating changes to their possible consequences for the controller. The starting point is a modification of the physical regime, operating range, or excitation relative to the conditions used to train the residual. Change may occur between trips, such as a different load, or during a run, such as a reduction in grip. Its actual values are used to define the plant and evaluation scenarios, but are not provided to the supervisor when the protocol declares them unknown.

Figure~\ref{fig:shift_validity_control_chain} summarizes this sequence and the feedback used during operation.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{imgs/residual_adaptation_block_diagram.png}
    \caption{Shift--validity--control chain and residual feedback in the proposed architecture.}
    \label{fig:shift_validity_control_chain}
\end{figure}
The first arrow does not imply that every change invalidates the learned correction. It indicates that the change may modify the discrepancy between B0 and the plant. Likewise, uncertainty and OOD signals do not constitute validity: they are candidate observations for constructing the confidence $C_k$ defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:runtime_confidence_signal}. Each link in the chain must be checked independently.

At time $k$, the controller has the measured or estimated signals forming $\mathbf z_k$. The learned block computes the correction $\hat{\mathbf d}_k$ and the available uncertainty components, while the OOD monitor characterizes departure from the training domain. The validity estimator transforms these signals into $C_k$ without necessarily receiving grip, mass, inertias, or tire parameters. Thus, the supervisor seeks to determine whether the correction retains sufficient support, rather than which physical parameter caused the change.

The signal $C_k$ modifies a controller configuration represented by $\boldsymbol{\eta}_k$:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\eta}_k=h(C_k),
    \qquad
    \mathbf u_k
    =
    \kappa_{\mathrm{MPC}}
    \!\left(
        \hat{\mathbf x}_k,
        \mathbf r_k,
        f_{\mathrm{hyb}},
        \boldsymbol{\eta}_k
    \right),
    \label{eq:validity_aware_control_law}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf r_k$ represents the reference and $f_{\mathrm{hyb}}$ the hybrid prediction model. The main mechanism to be investigated is horizon adaptation, so that $\boldsymbol{\eta}_k=N_k$ in the initial formulation. Function $h$, its bounds, and the appropriate direction of adaptation are not assumed in advance: they must be determined through the relationship between horizon, error accumulation, computational cost, and closed-loop performance.

After $\mathbf u_k$ is applied, the plant evolves and generates the signals used in the next cycle. These observations allow retrospective evaluation of residual error and the effect of the decision, but the action at time $k$ must be selected before they are known. This temporal separation is why $C_k$ is formulated as a confidence estimate rather than a direct measure of future error. In the thesis core, the learned model parameters remain frozen throughout the campaign; updating or retraining constitutes an optional extension.

Validation traverses the chain in both directions. First, it checks whether the selected changes alter the error and whether the available signals reflect that alteration. It then examines whether $C_k$ relates to control-relevant error and whether modifying the MPC/NMPC reduces degradation relative to architectures that do not monitor validity. A favorable result at one stage does not guarantee the next: detecting a change does not ensure it is harmful, estimating degradation does not ensure horizon adaptation is the appropriate response, and improving a predictive metric does not by itself demonstrate a closed-loop improvement. This structure makes each link a testable experimental hypothesis and defines the empirical scope of the conclusions.

\section{Hybrid Prediction Model}
\label{sec:arquitectura_modelo_hibrido}

The derivation of physical model B0 and its discretization is presented in Section~\ref{sec:modelos_orientados_control}; the definition of the residual target, its input variables, and the B1 prediction is given in Section~\ref{sec:modelos_hibridos_residuales}. On that basis, this section does not reformulate the individual models, but establishes the interface through which any of configurations B1--B4 is incorporated into the MPC/NMPC predictor.

Let $f_{\mathrm{B0}}$ be the nominal transition of the complete state $\mathbf{x}=[e_y,e_\psi,v_y,r]^{\mathsf T}$, and let $\bar{\mathbf d}^{(b)}(\mathbf z)$ be the mean correction provided by configuration $b$. Since the residual acts only on $v_y$ and $r$, its insertion into the complete state is expressed through
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{J}_d
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 & 0 \\
        0 & 0 \\
        1 & 0 \\
        0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    f_{\mathrm{hyb}}^{(b)}
    (\mathbf{x},\delta,\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}},\mathbf z)
    =
    f_{\mathrm{B0}}
    (\mathbf{x},\delta,\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}})
    +
    \mathbf{J}_d\bar{\mathbf d}^{(b)}(\mathbf z).
    \label{eq:full_state_hybrid_transition}
\end{equation}
This notation retains the evolution of tracking errors in B0 and adds the learned correction only to the two variables for which it was trained. It also allows the purely physical reference to be represented within the same interface through $\bar{\mathbf d}^{(0)}(\mathbf z)=\mathbf 0$; for $b\in\{1,2,3,4\}$, the correction corresponds to the point prediction or mean of the residual distribution of the model considered.

At each stage $i$ of the horizon, the learned block inputs are formed from the predicted variables available to the model:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf z_{i\mid k}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        V_{x,i\mid k} &
        \hat v_{y,i\mid k} &
        \hat r_{i\mid k} &
        \delta_{i\mid k}
    \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T},
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N_k-1.
    \label{eq:hybrid_rollout_features}
\end{equation}
Here, $V_{x,i\mid k}$ denotes the value available for prediction, either through a known reference or through the convention of holding the current measurement over the horizon. The specific choice must be fixed in the control protocol and kept identical when comparing B0--B4. Starting from the estimated initial state, the recursive rollout is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf x}_{0\mid k}=\hat{\mathbf x}_k,
    \qquad
    \hat{\mathbf x}_{i+1\mid k}
    =
    f_{\mathrm{hyb}}^{(b)}
    \!\left(
        \hat{\mathbf x}_{i\mid k},
        \delta_{i\mid k},
        \kappa_{\mathrm{ref},i\mid k},
        \mathbf z_{i\mid k}
    \right).
    \label{eq:hybrid_multistep_rollout}
\end{equation}
Thus, although the residual is trained for one-step prediction, the controller evaluates it recursively at points generated by its own predicted trajectory. The distinction between one-step error, its multistep accumulation, and its effect on the decision is developed in Section~\ref{sec:validez_relevante_control}.

The learned output is functionally divided into two paths. The residual mean $\bar{\mathbf d}^{(b)}$ modifies the transition in Equation~\eqref{eq:full_state_hybrid_transition}; the available uncertainty quantities are neither added to the state nor substituted for the correction. They are sent, together with the OOD indicator and calibration information, to the validity estimator. The interpretation of these quantities is established in Sections~\ref{sec:incertidumbre_predictiva} and \ref{sec:validez_confianza_operacion}, while their position within the complete architecture is shown in Section~\ref{sec:cadena_cambio_validez_control}. Thus, the MPC receives corrected dynamics for optimization and, through a separate path, a signal that may modify its configuration without confusing uncertainty with dynamics.

The boundary between available information and hidden physical parameters is established in Section~\ref{sec:regimen_fisico_no_representado}. Applied to Equation~\eqref{eq:full_state_hybrid_transition}, it means that $f_{\mathrm{B0}}$ uses nominal parameters and the learned block consumes only $\mathbf z_{i\mid k}$. Actual plant values are reserved for constructing scenarios and evaluating results; they are not part of the predictor interface. Thus, the comparison measures the contribution of the learned correction without providing the controller with privileged information about the physical change.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Comparison Protocol}

Inputs and targets are standardized using statistics calculated exclusively from the $3102$ training samples:
\begin{align}
    \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_k
    &=
    \mathbf{S}_z^{-1}
    \left(\mathbf{z}_k-\boldsymbol{\mu}_z\right),
    \label{eq:b1_input_normalization}\\
    \tilde{\mathbf{d}}_k
    &=
    \mathbf{S}_d^{-1}
    \left(\mathbf{d}_k-\boldsymbol{\mu}_d\right).
    \label{eq:b1_target_normalization}
\end{align}
Vectors $\boldsymbol{\mu}_z$ and $\boldsymbol{\mu}_d$ contain the training means, while $\mathbf{S}_z$ and $\mathbf{S}_d$ are diagonal matrices containing the corresponding standard deviations. The same transformations are applied to validation and ID test data without refitting. A normalized prediction is returned to physical units through
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{d}}_k
    =
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_d
    +
    \mathbf{S}_d\hat{\tilde{\mathbf{d}}}_k.
    \label{eq:b1_denormalization}
\end{equation}

Predictive ability is evaluated for each residual component using RMSE and MAE. In addition, the one-step hybrid prediction is compared with B0 on the same ID test transitions. Uncertainty and calibration metrics are added for probabilistic models, but are defined in the corresponding model-specific sections. Scenarios outside the nominal domain are not involved in training or selection and are reserved for studying loss of validity.

\subsection{B1: Deterministic Residual MLP}
\label{sec:modelo_b1}

B1 is the deterministic residual baseline. Its role is to establish how much the B0 error can be reduced through a neural point correction, without yet incorporating a predictive distribution, uncertainty decomposition, or validity monitoring mechanism. B1 outputs an estimate of the two one-step residual components.

The MLP receives four standardized inputs, contains two hidden layers of 64 neurons with ReLU activation, and produces two linear outputs. In compact form,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\tilde{\mathbf{d}}}_k
    =
    \mathbf{W}_3
    \rho\!\left(
        \mathbf{W}_2
        \rho\!\left(
            \mathbf{W}_1\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_k+\mathbf{b}_1
        \right)
        +\mathbf{b}_2
    \right)
    +\mathbf{b}_3,
    \label{eq:b1_mlp_mapping}
\end{equation}
where $\rho(a)=\max(0,a)$ is applied componentwise. The output is destandardized through Equation~\eqref{eq:b1_denormalization} and added directly to the physical prediction, according to Equation~\eqref{eq:b1_hybrid_prediction}.

Fitting is performed with MATLAB's \texttt{fitrnet} function. Limits of 100 and 200 iterations are compared, and the candidate with the lowest joint validation RMSE in standardized space is selected:
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathrm{val}}
    =
    \sqrt{
        \frac{1}{2N_{\mathrm{val}}}
        \sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{val}}}
        \left\|
            \tilde{\mathbf{d}}_k
            -
            \hat{\tilde{\mathbf{d}}}_k
        \right\|_2^2
    }.
    \label{eq:b1_validation_score}
\end{equation}
The 200-iteration limit achieved $J_{\mathrm{val}}=0.073810$, compared with $0.075873$ for 100 iterations, and was the retained configuration. Table~\ref{tab:b1_configuration} summarizes only the decisions specific to B1.

\subsection{B2: Homoscedastic Gaussian Process}
\label{sec:modelo_b2}

B2 replaces the deterministic MLP in B1 with two independent Gaussian processes (GPs), one for $d_{v_y}$ and one for $d_r$. Its role in the study is to retain correction ability comparable to B1 while adding a predictive distribution that quantifies uncertainty within and outside the nominal domain. Independence between the two outputs simplifies training, but means that B2 does not explicitly model their cross-covariance.

For each component $j\in\{v_y,r\}$, consider
\begin{equation}
    d_{j,k}
    =
    f_j(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_k)
    +
    \varepsilon_{j,k},
    \qquad
    \varepsilon_{j,k}\sim
    \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n,j}^2),
    \label{eq:b2_gp_observation_model}
\end{equation}
where $f_j$ follows an exact GP with a squared-exponential kernel and automatic relevance determination (ARD). For standardized inputs, the kernel can be written as
\begin{equation}
    k_j(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}')
    =
    \sigma_{f,j}^2
    \exp\!\left[
        -\frac{1}{2}
        \sum_{q=1}^{4}
        \frac{(z_q-z'_q)^2}{\ell_{j,q}^2}
    \right],
    \label{eq:b2_ard_kernel}
\end{equation}
with one length scale $\ell_{j,q}$ per input. The term $\sigma_{n,j}$ is constant throughout the input space; this assumption defines the homoscedastic nature of B2.

The predictive distribution of each output is represented as
\begin{equation}
    p(d_{j,*}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_*,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}})
    =
    \mathcal{N}\!\left(
        \mu_{j,*},
        \sigma_{\mathrm{epi},j,*}^{2}+\sigma_{n,j}^{2}
    \right).
    \label{eq:b2_predictive_distribution}
\end{equation}
Here, $\sigma_{\mathrm{epi},j,*}$ is the posterior standard deviation of the latent function and decreases near informative data; $\sigma_{n,j}$ represents a learned constant dispersion. Thus, B2 provides total predictive uncertainty and an approximation of the epistemic component, but cannot represent local variations in aleatoric dispersion.

Both GPs are trained on the common partition, without additional data search or changes to normalization. Table~\ref{tab:b2_configuration} summarizes the specific decisions and times recorded in the training artifact.

\subsection{B3: Deep Ensemble of Heteroscedastic MLPs}
\label{sec:modelo_b3}

B3 uses a \textit{deep ensemble} of five heteroscedastic MLPs. Unlike B1, each member simultaneously predicts the mean and conditional variance of the two residual components. Unlike B2, aleatoric dispersion can vary with the input, and epistemic uncertainty is approximated through disagreement among independently trained networks.

Each member $m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}$ receives $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_k$ and produces four outputs:
\begin{equation}
    \left[
        \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(m)}_k,
        \mathbf{s}^{(m)}_k
    \right]
    =
    \mathbf{g}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_m}
    (\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_k),
    \qquad
    \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(m)}_k,\mathbf{s}^{(m)}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{2},
    \label{eq:b3_member_output}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(m)}_k$ is the normalized residual mean and $\mathbf{s}^{(m)}_k$ represents log-variance. To maintain training stability, its components are restricted to $[-8,5]$ and transformed through
\begin{equation}
    \left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}\right)^{(m)}_k
    =
    \exp\!\left(\mathbf{s}^{(m)}_k\right).
    \label{eq:b3_member_variance}
\end{equation}

Each network is fitted in normalized space by minimizing the heteroscedastic Gaussian negative log-likelihood (NLL). Omitting constants independent of the parameters, the loss for one member is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}^{(m)}_{mathrm{NLL}}
    =
    \frac{1}{2N}
    \sum_{k=1}^{N}
    \sum_{j=1}^{2}
    \left[
        s^{(m)}_{j,k}
        +
        \exp\!\left(-s^{(m)}_{j,k}\right)
        \left(
            \tilde d_{j,k}-\mu^{(m)}_{j,k}
        \right)^2
    \right].
    \label{eq:b3_heteroscedastic_nll}
\end{equation}
This function requires each member to balance mean error against the dispersion assigned to each sample; a large variance reduces the quadratic penalty but directly increases the log-variance term.

The joint prediction is obtained by combining the moments of the $M=5$ members. For each residual component,
\begin{align}
    \bar{\mu}_k
    &=
    \frac{1}{M}
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}\mu^{(m)}_k,
    \label{eq:b3_ensemble_mean}\\
    U^2_{\mathrm{epi},k}
    &=
    \frac{1}{M}
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \left(\mu^{(m)}_k-\bar{\mu}_k\right)^2,
    \label{eq:b3_epistemic}\\
    U^2_{\mathrm{ale},k}
    &=
    \frac{1}{M}
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \left(\sigma^2\right)^{(m)}_k,
    \label{eq:b3_aleatoric}\\
    U_{\mathrm{tot},k}
    &=
    \sqrt{
        U^2_{\mathrm{epi},k}
        +
        U^2_{\mathrm{ale},k}
    }.
    \label{eq:b3_total_uncertainty}
\end{align}
Means and standard deviations are then transformed into physical units using the common target statistics. $U_{\mathrm{ale}}$ must be interpreted as an operational estimate of learned conditional dispersion, not as a direct, pure measurement of physical noise.

Each MLP retains B1's two hidden layers of 64 neurons, but its output layer has four components. Training uses batches of 256 samples, a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, a maximum of 250 epochs, and early stopping with a patience of 25 epochs. Base seed 42 controls reproducible member initialization; it does not define the data split, which remains fixed by Table~\ref{tab:common_frozen_split}. Training was performed on CPU. Table~\ref{tab:b3_configuration} summarizes these decisions.

\subsection{B4: Two-Stage Heteroscedastic Gaussian Process}
\label{sec:modelo_b4}

B4 retains B2's two mean GPs unchanged and adds a second stage to estimate input-dependent residual dispersion. This construction isolates the effect of heteroscedasticity: B2 and B4 share the same predictive mean and latent epistemic uncertainty, while differing in their treatment of residual dispersion, constant in B2 and variable in B4.

The first stage is defined by the exact GPs with squared-exponential ARD kernels described for B2. To train the second stage without using residuals obtained on the same samples used to fit the mean, out-of-fold predictions are generated within the training partition. If $g(i)$ identifies the group containing sample $i$, the corresponding residual is
\begin{equation}
    e^{\mathrm{OOF}}_{j,i}
    =
    d_{j,i}
    -
    \hat{\mu}^{(-g(i))}_{j}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_i),
    \qquad j\in\{v_y,r\},
    \label{eq:b4_oof_residual}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mu}^{(-g(i))}_{j}$ is obtained without using the group of the evaluated sample. The RMSEs of these out-of-fold predictions were $0.010188\,\mathrm{m/s}$ for $d_{v_y}$ and $0.008365\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ for $d_r$.

For each output, a second GP is fitted to a logarithmic transformation of the squared out-of-fold residual. The training artifact retains the correction $c_{\chi^2}=\mathbb{E}[\log(\chi_1^2)]=-1.27036$, used to compensate for the bias introduced by the logarithmic transformation. In compact form, this stage's target can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    q_{j,i}
    =
    \log\!\left[
        \left(e^{\mathrm{OOF}}_{j,i}\right)^2+\epsilon_j
    \right]
    -c_{\chi^2},
    \label{eq:b4_variance_target}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_j>0$ avoids the logarithm's singularity. If $\hat q_j(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_*)$ is the dispersion GP prediction, the operational uncertainty components are obtained as
\begin{align}
    U^2_{\mathrm{ale},j,*}
    &=
    \exp\!\left[\hat q_j(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_*)\right],
    \label{eq:b4_aleatoric}\\
    U_{\mathrm{epi},j,*}
    &=
    \sigma_{f,j,*},
    \label{eq:b4_epistemic}\\
    U_{\mathrm{tot},j,*}
    &=
    \sqrt{
        U^2_{\mathrm{epi},j,*}
        +
        U^2_{\mathrm{ale},j,*}
    },
    \label{eq:b4_total_uncertainty}
\end{align}
where $\sigma_{f,j,*}$ is the posterior standard deviation of the mean GP's latent function. These quantities are presented in physical units after reversing the common standardization.

The designation $U_{\mathrm{ale}}$ is retained to maintain a uniform taxonomy across models, but its interpretation requires caution. Because it is learned from $e^{\mathrm{OOF}}$, this component may encompass irreducible noise, mean-model error, and variability between groups. In B4, it therefore represents a \emph{heteroscedastic estimate of residual dispersion}, not a pure measurement of physical random noise.

\subsection{Nominal Reference and Excitation Generalization: E0--E1}

E0 establishes the performance level within the known distribution. Although it retains DLC and DLCT maneuvers, nominal speed, grip, and vehicle A, it uses complete runs not involved in fitting. E0 therefore measures generalization across nominal runs, rather than performance on training samples. Its errors and uncertainties serve as the reference for interpreting all increases observed in E1--E9.

E1 retains $V_x=60\,\mathrm{km/h}$, $\mu=1$, and vehicle A, but replaces the identification maneuvers with a single SLC maneuver. This isolation evaluates generalization to an unseen excitation pattern without simultaneously introducing a change in physical parameters. The main comparison is E1 against E0.

\subsection{Observable Operating Point Shifts: E2--E3}

E2 and E3 evaluate extrapolation relative to the sole longitudinal speed present in training. E2 reduces $V_x$ from 60 to $50\,\mathrm{km/h}$, and E3 increases it to $70\,\mathrm{km/h}$, retaining $\mu=1$, vehicle A, and the DLC and DLCT maneuver families. Because $V_x$ is an input to B1--B4, these scenarios constitute observable covariate shifts and allow checking whether the probabilistic models' epistemic uncertainty responds to departure from the nominal domain. Separate evaluation of E2 and E3 also identifies any asymmetry between extrapolating toward lower speeds and toward a more demanding regime.

\subsection{Latent Dynamic Changes in Grip: E4--E6}

E4--E6 form a sequence of increasing severity in which $V_x=60\,\mathrm{km/h}$, vehicle A, and DLC and DLCT maneuvers are retained while $\mu$ decreases from its nominal value of 1.0 to 0.8, 0.6, and 0.3. Since $\mu$ is not part of the input vector, these scenarios modify the plant's input--output relationship without providing the model with a direct indication of the cause.

E4 represents a moderate perturbation; E5, a strong change; and E6, an extreme test. Joint analysis of the three scenarios determines whether error increases monotonically with the physical discrepancy and whether $U_{\mathrm{epi}}$, $U_{\mathrm{ale}}$, or $U_{\mathrm{tot}}$ track that increase. E6 is used specifically to search for confident failures: intervals in which error increases without a proportional rise in uncertainty.

\subsection{Combined Maneuver and Grip Change: E7}

E7 combines the two modifications previously isolated: it uses the unseen SLC maneuver and reduces grip to $\mu=0.6$, retaining $V_x=60\,\mathrm{km/h}$ and vehicle A. The natural references for interpretation are E1, which contains only the maneuver change, and E5, which contains only the same grip change. This comparison examines whether the joint degradation can be explained by the isolated changes or whether an additional interaction between excitation and dynamics appears.

\subsection{Structural Vehicle Change: E8}

E8 retains nominal maneuver, speed, and grip conditions, but replaces vehicle A with vehicle B. The scenario groups structural variations in mass, inertias, and tire properties that are not explicitly included in the learned models' input vector. Direct comparison with E0 studies sensitivity to a change of platform without confounding it with speed, surface, or maneuver variations. As in the grip scenarios, actual values of modified parameters are used to define and analyze the test, but are not provided as inputs to B1--B4.

\subsection{Abrupt Change during Execution: E9}

E9 introduces a temporal dimension absent from the preceding scenarios. During a continuous DLC/DLCT-based run, $\mu$ changes abruptly from 1.0 to 0.6, while $V_x=60\,\mathrm{km/h}$ and vehicle A are retained. Rather than only summarizing performance over the entire run, this scenario is analyzed around the transition instant. The objective is to characterize the temporal evolution of error and uncertainties, including any delay between physical change, predictive degradation, and the response of available indicators. E0 and E5 provide the stationary references before and after the change, respectively.

\section{Learned Model and Uncertainty}
\label{sec:modelo_aprendido_incertidumbre_metodologia}

The structures, fitting criteria, and nominal-domain results of B1--B4 are presented in Section~\ref{sec:descripcion_modelos_entrenados}. At this methodological stage, the models are not retrained: they are used as frozen artifacts to generate the residual correction and available uncertainty signals on the same transitions of each scenario in Section~\ref{sec:escenarios_cambio_distribucion}. Thus, any difference between scenarios is attributed to the condition evaluated, rather than predictor readaptation.

For each model $b$, scenario $s$, and instant $k$, a common record is retained,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}^{(b,s)}_k
    =
    \left\{
        \mathbf z_k,
        \mathbf d_k,
        \bar{\mathbf d}^{(b)}_k,
        \mathbf e^{(b)}_{d,k},
        \mathbf U^{(b)}_{\mathrm{epi},k},
        \mathbf U^{(b)}_{\mathrm{ale},k},
        \mathbf U^{(b)}_{\mathrm{tot},k},
        S_{\mathrm{OOD},k},
        t^{(b)}_{\mathrm{inf},k}
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:common_uncertainty_record}
\end{equation}
The observed discrepancy $\mathbf d_k$ and error $\mathbf e^{(b)}_{d,k}$, defined according to Equation~\eqref{eq:physics_hybrid_residual_errors}, are calculated after receiving the CarSim transition and used only as ground truth for evaluation. They are not among the signals available when taking the control action. Time $t^{(b)}_{\mathrm{inf},k}$ includes mean prediction and, where applicable, computation of its uncertainty components.

B1 provides only $\bar{\mathbf d}^{(1)}_k$ and acts as the deterministic reference; its uncertainty fields are recorded as unavailable, rather than zero. B2--B4 provide a mean and a probabilistic characterization, with the specific definitions in Sections~\ref{sec:modelo_b2}, \ref{sec:modelo_b3}, and \ref{sec:modelo_b4}. This distinction avoids interpreting the absence of an estimate as perfect certainty. The general semantics of $U_{\mathrm{epi}}$, $U_{\mathrm{ale}}$, and $U_{\mathrm{tot}}$ are established in Section~\ref{sec:incertidumbre_predictiva}; differences between their estimators remain visible throughout the analysis and are not presented as equivalent physical measurements.

The two residual outputs have different units and scales. Therefore, before forming scalar indicators, each uncertainty component is normalized by the target standard deviation calculated on the training partition, namely,
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde U^{(b)}_{q,j,k}
    =
    \frac{U^{(b)}_{q,j,k}}{s_{d_j,\mathrm{tr}}},
    \qquad
    \widetilde U^{(b)}_{q,k}
    =
    \sqrt{
        \frac{1}{2}
        \sum_{j\in\{v_y,r\}}
        \left(\widetilde U^{(b)}_{q,j,k}\right)^2
    },
    \quad
    q\in\{\mathrm{epi},\mathrm{ale},\mathrm{tot}\}.
    \label{eq:dimensionless_uncertainty_aggregation}
\end{equation}
Componentwise signals are retained for diagnostics; aggregation is used only when the supervisor requires a scalar input. This prevents an output from dominating the combination because of its unit or numerical magnitude. Since B2--B4 construct uncertainty through different mechanisms, their main comparison is performed both in absolute terms and relative to each model's E0 reference.

Calibration is evaluated without using E1--E9 to modify the predictive distributions. If a recalibration transformation is applied, its parameters are estimated only on the validation partition described in Section~\ref{sec:entorno_comun_modelos}, after fixing the predictor configuration, and remain frozen during the ID test and shift campaign. Coverage and NLL are calculated per output and scenario according to Equations~\eqref{eq:empirical_predictive_coverage} and \eqref{eq:gaussian_predictive_nll}. This separation checks whether calibration obtained under nominal conditions is retained, becomes conservative, or produces overconfidence under each family of change.

An OOD score computed in standardized input space is used as complementary information. The reference option is a regularized Mahalanobis distance relative to the training data:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{OOD},k}
    =
    \sqrt{
        \left(\widetilde{\mathbf z}_k-
              \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\widetilde z,\mathrm{tr}}\right)^{\mathsf T}
        \left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\widetilde z,\mathrm{tr}}
              +\lambda\mathbf I\right)^{-1}
        \left(\widetilde{\mathbf z}_k-
              \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\widetilde z,\mathrm{tr}}\right)
    },
    \qquad \lambda>0.
    \label{eq:mahalanobis_ood_score}
\end{equation}
The mean, covariance, regularization, and any threshold are determined without using the test scenarios and then kept fixed. The inverse matrix can be computed before operation, so online evaluation requires only operations on four inputs. This score detects departure in $\mathbf z_k$, but is not assumed capable of revealing latent dynamic changes on its own, such as a grip variation that does not shift the observed covariates.

In E0--E8, analysis is performed per run and then aggregated by scenario, avoiding treating correlated transitions as independent repetitions. In E9, time series synchronized with the change instant are also retained. Any smoothing used by the supervisor must be causal, depend only on the current and previous samples, and retain the same parameters across all scenarios. Post-change error is reserved as an evaluation label and is never used to anticipate the uncertainty or OOD signal.

Finally, the computational cost of each block is recorded through the distribution of $t^{(b)}_{\mathrm{inf},k}$ and compared with sampling period $T_s$. This check is performed with the models already loaded and, when necessary, separates the time spent on the mean, uncertainty, and OOD score. Only signals computable before applying $\mathbf u_k$, calibrated without test-scenario information, and compatible with the execution deadline proceed to the construction of $C_k$.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metricas_evaluacion}

Metrics are first calculated for each complete run and then aggregated by scenario. Consecutive transitions are not considered independent repetitions because they share a trajectory, initial state, and temporal evolution. When two architectures are evaluated on the same CarSim run, the comparison is paired. Results are presented per component and family of change; a single average over E0--E9 is used only as a secondary summary, since it could hide failures concentrated in a particular regime.

\subsection{Model Metrics}
\label{sec:metricas_modelo}

One-step evaluation uses the residual error in Equation~\eqref{eq:physics_hybrid_residual_errors}. For component $j\in\{v_y,r\}$, model $b$, and run $q$ with $n_q$ transitions, the following are calculated:
\begin{align}
    \operatorname{RMSE}^{(b,q)}_j
    &=
    \sqrt{
        \frac{1}{n_q}
        \sum_{k=1}^{n_q}
        \left(e^{(b)}_{d,j,k}\right)^2
    },
    \label{eq:evaluation_rmse}\\
    \operatorname{MAE}^{(b,q)}_j
    &=
    \frac{1}{n_q}
    \sum_{k=1}^{n_q}
    \left|e^{(b)}_{d,j,k}\right|.
    \label{eq:evaluation_mae}
\end{align}
RMSE penalizes large errors more strongly, while MAE checks that the comparison does not depend on a few extreme samples. Both are retained in physical units. To quantify the residual's contribution relative to B0, the relative RMSE reduction is also reported,
\begin{equation}
    I^{(b,q)}_{\mathrm{RMSE},j}
    =
    100\left(
        1-
        \frac{\operatorname{RMSE}^{(b,q)}_j}
             {\operatorname{RMSE}^{(\mathrm{B0},q)}_j}
    \right),
    \label{eq:relative_rmse_improvement}
\end{equation}
expressed as a percentage. A positive value represents improvement, and a negative value indicates that the learned correction harms prediction. This measure complements the instantaneous gain defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:one_step_residual_gain} and allows comparison of the residual's average usefulness across scenarios.

Multistep behavior is evaluated using the state error in Equation~\eqref{eq:multistep_state_prediction_error}. For a horizon $H$, the following is used:
\begin{equation}
    E^{(b,q)}_{H}
    =
    \sqrt{
        \frac{1}{H}
        \sum_{i=1}^{H}
        \left\|
            \mathbf e^{(b,q)}_{x,i\mid k}
        \right\|_{\mathbf W_x}^{2}
    },
    \label{eq:weighted_multistep_error}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf W_x$ contains fixed scales to prevent states with different units or magnitudes from dominating the result. Errors are also reported per state and step $i$, because the aggregate value does not show whether degradation appears at the beginning or end of the horizon. To isolate model quality, B0--B4 receive the same input sequence in this test; differences caused by the actions selected by each controller are subsequently evaluated in closed loop.

\subsection{Uncertainty, Calibration, and Out-of-Distribution Detection Metrics}
\label{sec:metricas_incertidumbre_ood}

The probabilistic quality of B2--B4 is characterized through the coverage and NLL defined in Equations~\eqref{eq:empirical_predictive_coverage} and \eqref{eq:gaussian_predictive_nll}. B3's moment-matching convention is retained across all scenarios so that its values are comparable with B2 and B4. Because coverage at a single level may favor unnecessarily wide intervals, several nominal levels $\mathcal{P}=\{p_1,\ldots,p_L\}$ are evaluated, and the mean calibration error is calculated as
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{CE}^{(b,s)}_j
    =
    \frac{1}{L}
    \sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
    \left|
        \widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}^{(b,s)}_j(p_\ell)-p_\ell
    \right|.
    \label{eq:mean_calibration_error}
\end{equation}
This result is accompanied by mean interval width. This distinguishes a well-calibrated, sharp distribution from one that obtains coverage through excessive dispersion.

The usefulness of uncertainties as error indicators is evaluated separately for $U_{\mathrm{epi}}$, $U_{\mathrm{ale}}$, and $U_{\mathrm{tot}}$. Spearman correlation with $|e_{d,j,k}|$ is reported both per output and for the dimensionless quantities in Equation~\eqref{eq:dimensionless_uncertainty_aggregation}. Correlation is complemented by the confident failure rate,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{FCR}^{(b,s)}_j
    =
    \frac{
        \sum_k
        \mathbb I\!\left\{
            |e^{(b)}_{d,j,k}|>\tau_{e,j},\;
            U^{(b)}_{\mathrm{tot},j,k}\leq\tau_{U,j}
        \right\}
    }{
        \sum_k
        \mathbb I\!\left\{
            |e^{(b)}_{d,j,k}|>\tau_{e,j}
        \right\}
    },
    \label{eq:confident_failure_rate}
\end{equation}
which represents the fraction of high errors accompanied by uncertainty that is not high. Thresholds $\tau_{e,j}$ and $\tau_{U,j}$ are fixed using quantiles of the nominal validation partition and are not retuned for each scenario. If no error exceeds $\tau_{e,j}$, the rate is declared undefined rather than assigned a zero.

The score in Equation~\eqref{eq:mahalanobis_ood_score} is evaluated using E0 as the negative reference and each shift scenario as the positive set. AUROC, AUPR, and the false-positive rate at the selected sensitivity level are reported. These metrics are broken down by scenario: combining them into a single OOD class would hide the difference between observable covariate shifts and latent dynamics changes. For the latter, a low AUROC does not necessarily contradict the functioning of the input score; it indicates that the change is not identifiable through $\mathbf z_k$ and must be considered when assessing complementarity with uncertainties.

A temporal evaluation is added for E9. Given a causal indicator $a_k$, a threshold $\gamma_a$, and a minimum persistence of $L_a$ samples, detection delay is
\begin{equation}
    T^{(a)}_{\mathrm{det}}
    =
    T_s\left(
        \min\left\{
            k\geq k_{\mathrm{shift}}:
            a_{k:k+L_a-1}>\gamma_a
        \right\}
        -k_{\mathrm{shift}}
    \right).
    \label{eq:shift_detection_delay}
\end{equation}
The threshold and persistence are fixed before evaluating E9. Alongside delay, the false-alarm rate in the pre-change segment and the proportion of runs with no detection are reported. Thus, a rapid response is not rewarded if it comes at the expense of frequent alarms.

The execution cost of monitoring is summarized through the median, 95th percentile, and maximum of the times recorded in Equation~\eqref{eq:common_uncertainty_record}. The deadline miss rate is also calculated,
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{miss}}
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{k=1}^{N}
    \mathbb I\!\left\{t_{\mathrm{total},k}>T_s\right\},
    \label{eq:deadline_miss_rate}
\end{equation}
where $t_{\mathrm{total},k}$ includes prediction, uncertainty, OOD, and validity signal construction. Time is measured after loading the models, without mixing initialization with periodic inference.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Performance Metrics}
\label{sec:metricas_lazo_cerrado}

Closed-loop metrics constitute the main criterion for determining whether validity estimation provides value to the controller. The RMSE and maximum absolute values of $e_y$ and $e_\psi$ are calculated for each run. The former measures sustained behavior, while the latter preserves transient errors that might disappear in the average. Dynamic states $v_y$ and $r$ are summarized through RMS and absolute maxima to check that improved tracking does not come at the expense of excessive lateral responses.

Overall performance is also expressed through a realized cost using the same weights as the predictive problem in Equation~\eqref{eq:mpc_cost}:
\begin{equation}
    J^{(q)}_{\mathrm{cl}}
    =
    \frac{1}{n_q}
    \sum_{k=0}^{n_q-1}
    \left(
        \left\|\mathbf x_k-\mathbf x^{\mathrm{ref}}_k\right\|_{\mathbf Q}^{2}
        +
        \left\|\mathbf u_k\right\|_{\mathbf R}^{2}
    \right).
    \label{eq:realized_closed_loop_cost}
\end{equation}
This cost facilitates scalar comparison, but does not replace reporting its components. Steering effort is characterized through the RMS and maximum of $\delta_k$, and its variation through
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{TV}^{(q)}_{\delta}
    =
    \sum_{k=1}^{n_q-1}
    |\delta_k-\delta_{k-1}|.
    \label{eq:steering_total_variation}
\end{equation}
This latter metric detects oscillations or control activity that an average cost might conceal.

Constraints are analyzed without reducing them to a binary variable. For each scalar constraint written as $g_c(\mathbf x_k,\mathbf u_k)\leq0$, the fraction of violating samples, maximum magnitude, and accumulated violation are recorded,
\begin{equation}
    V^{(q)}_c
    =
    \sum_{k=0}^{n_q-1}
    \max\!\left\{0,g_c(\mathbf x_k,\mathbf u_k)\right\}T_s.
    \label{eq:accumulated_constraint_violation}
\end{equation}
The proportion of runs with at least one violation is also reported. This prevents many successful runs from hiding a severe failure under a specific condition.

Computational feasibility is evaluated through the rate of successfully solved optimization problems, the number of fallback action activations, median solution time, its 95th percentile and maximum, and the rate of cycles exceeding $T_s$. These indicators are presented alongside horizon $N_k$, so that any tracking improvement can be related to the cost of MPC/NMPC adaptation.

Post-change recovery is also measured in E9. Recovery time is the interval from $k_{\mathrm{shift}}$ until tracking error again remains within a band fixed from E0 for $L_{\mathrm{rec}}$ samples. If the run ends before satisfying the criterion, it is recorded as not recovered; it is not replaced with the maximum observed duration. The maximum post-change error and accumulated cost during recovery are also reported.

The primary outcomes are the RMSE and maximum of $e_y$, constraint violations, optimizer feasibility, and compliance with the sampling deadline. Heading error, dynamic states, control effort, steering variation, and recovery time serve as complementary outcomes. This hierarchy is fixed before the final comparison to avoid selecting only metrics favorable to an architecture after the fact.
